From SLI to NVSwitchThe Technologies We Had to Kill to Build AI‑Scale Networking

Modern AI isn’t just a software revolution. It’s a hardware miracle built on the bones of technologies we had to aggressively kill.

PGJMJCAS

By Piyush Gupta, Jeet Mane, Jai Chavan & Atharva Sheramkar

October 2026 · 7 min read · 4 legacy technologies, buried

When you spend your late nights studying computer architecture and systems design, you quickly realize a humbling truth: modern AI isn’t just a software revolution. It’s a hardware miracle built on the bones of technologies we had to aggressively kill.

For years, we treated computing as a game of raw compute power. But as generative models exploded, we hit a wall. GPUs became 1,000× faster, but networks only managed a 10× speedup. AI scaling stopped being a pure compute problem and turned into a massive data movement problem. To build clusters capable of training hundreds of billions of parameters, the tech industry had to throw out decades of legacy assumptions.

Here is a look back at the graveyard of legacy technologies and the modern marvels that replaced them.

01

Part 01 / 04 · Multi-GPU

Ditching SLI & the Myth of Master‑Slave Multi‑GPU

DeprecatedSLI & CrossFire
SuccessorNVLink & NVSwitch

What we left behind

SLI and CrossFire

Back in the golden age of PC gaming, NVIDIA’s SLI (Scalable Link Interface) and AMD’s CrossFire were the holy grail for enthusiast builds. They used Split-Frame or Alternate-Frame Rendering to tie two graphics cards together. But there was a major catch: SLI forced one GPU to act as a “master” gathering rendered frames from “slave” GPUs, and it completely duplicated VRAM. If you ran two 8 GB cards in SLI, you still only had 8 GB of usable memory.

Why it failed for AI

Memory has to pool, not mirror

Large Language Models cannot fit into a single GPU’s memory. Master-slave rendering and VRAM duplication are utterly useless when thousands of matrix operations need to pool memory in parallel, not duplicate it.

The modern replacement

NVLink and NVSwitch

Modern AI relies on NVLink to abandon display-oriented logic entirely. Instead of passing pixels, it enables direct memory load and store operations between GPU VRAM spaces at multi-terabyte-per-second speeds, creating a unified memory pool where multiple GPUs act as a single logical processor.

02

Part 02 / 04 · The Bus

The Death of PCIe as the Primary Bus

DeprecatedThe CPU root complex
SuccessorCustom silicon fabrics

What we left behind

Relying on the CPU root complex

Traditionally, if GPU 1 wanted to talk to GPU 2, the data had to travel up the PCIe lane to the CPU and system RAM, traverse the PCIe root complex, and travel back down to GPU 2.

Why it failed for AI

An 800B-parameter traffic jam

A PCIe Gen5 x16 slot tops out at roughly 64 GB/s bi-directional throughput. When you are distributing an 800B parameter model across multiple cards, routing everything through the CPU creates a catastrophic bottleneck.

The modern replacement

Custom silicon fabrics (NVSwitch)

Modern AI servers bypass PCIe for GPU-to-GPU traffic completely. Inside a DGX node or an NVLink domain (like the GB200 NVL72), GPUs are glued together via custom crossbar switch chips, delivering up to 1.8 TB/s of bi-directional bandwidth per GPU.

03

Part 03 / 04 · The Stack

Throwing Out the TCP/IP Stack

DeprecatedKernel packet processing
SuccessorGPUDirect RDMA

What we left behind

Kernel-level packet processing

For decades, networking meant the standard OS TCP/IP stack. Every incoming packet triggered CPU interrupts and multiple memory copies (from NIC buffer to OS kernel space, to user RAM, and finally to GPU VRAM).

Why it failed for AI

The CPU as a high-priced bottleneck

At line rates of 400 Gbps or 800 Gbps, CPU memory buses choke instantly, and CPU cores burn out just parsing packet headers. The CPU becomes a high-priced bottleneck for data it doesn’t even need to touch.

The modern replacement

GPUDirect RDMA (GDR)

GPUDirect RDMA completely bypasses the host CPU, operating system, and system RAM. The remote NIC writes data directly across the PCIe switch straight into local GPU VRAM in a true zero-copy architecture.

04

Part 04 / 04 · The Fabric

Lossless Networking (InfiniBand vs. RoCEv2)

Deprecated“Best-effort” Ethernet
SuccessorInfiniBand & RoCEv2

What we left behind

Standard “best-effort” Ethernet

Standard Ethernet deals with network congestion by dropping packets and relying on TCP to retransmit them later.

Why it failed for AI

One straggler stalls them all

AI training depends heavily on synchronous parallel algorithms like AllReduce. If 1 out of 10,000 GPUs drops a single packet and hits a 10 ms retransmission timeout, the other 9,999 GPUs are forced to pause and idle, waiting for that single straggler.

The modern replacement

InfiniBand & RoCEv2

To prevent the dreaded straggler problem, modern clusters use credit-based flow control (InfiniBand) or Priority Flow Control (RoCEv2) to create a strictly lossless network fabric where packets are never dropped due to buffer congestion.

InfiniBand

Credit-based flow control

A sender only transmits when the receiver has advertised buffer credits. No room, no send — so nothing overflows.

RoCEv2

Priority Flow Control

RDMA over Ethernet. When a buffer fills, PFC pauses that traffic class upstream instead of dropping packets.

05

Conclusion

What’s Next?

Looking back at this graveyard, it’s wild to see how much legacy baggage we had to drop to make generative AI a reality.

  • Master-slave VRAM duplicationkilled
  • The CPU root complex detourrouted around
  • The TCP/IP kernel stacktossed out
  • Dropped packetsoutlawed

The big question now is: Is Ethernet finally ready to reclaim the data center from InfiniBand? With the rise of the Ultra Ethernet Consortium, engineers are trying to bring lossless, high-performance scale-out capabilities back to standard Ethernet. As a student watching this unfold, I can’t wait to see if Ethernet can pull off a redemption arc—or if custom fabrics will continue to rule the AI world.
